## Who he is

Neil deGrasse Tyson is a native New Yorker who went to public schools and graduated from the Bronx High School of Science. He earned a bachelor's degree in physics from Harvard University and a PhD in astrophysics from Columbia University,[^1] in 1991.[^2]

As a 17-year-old with an interest in science, he was invited by Carl Sagan to visit Sagan's laboratory at Cornell. That early encouragement still inspires him.[^2]

## His contribution to skepticism

Tyson's public role began when he took over as director of the Hayden Planetarium in New York, in 1995. He played a key role in designing its Rose Center for Earth and Space.[^1]

- **Pluto.** When Tyson and his colleagues designed the Rose Center in 2000, they put Pluto "with a group of newly discovered icy objects in the outer solar system," instead of with the planets. In January 2001, *The New York Times* ran the story on its front page under the headline "Pluto's Not a Planet? Only in New York." Angry letters followed, "especially from pissed off third-graders."[^3] Tyson's point was about the evidence: "I never wanted to kick Pluto out of the solar system. I just wanted to group it with its icy brethren." In 2006, the International Astronomical Union adopted a definition saying a planet must clear the neighborhood around its orbit, and Pluto was no longer a planet.[^3]
- **Cosmos.** He was the executive editor and host of *Cosmos: A Spacetime Odyssey* (2014), a 13-episode, Emmy-nominated remake of [Carl Sagan](/skeptics/carl-sagan)'s 1980 series.[^1]
- **StarTalk and books.** He hosts *StarTalk Radio*, a show and podcast about "all things space," and has written books including *The Pluto Files*.[^1]
- **Science literacy.** He worries about how little science many adults know. The big public questions, he argues, "all require some level of science literacy in order to be an informed electorate."[^2]

In 2015, the National Academy of Sciences gave him its Public Welfare Medal, its most prestigious award, for his "extraordinary role in exciting the public about the wonders of science, from atoms to the Universe." Sagan had received the same medal.[^1]
