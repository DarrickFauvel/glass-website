## Who she is

Emily Rosa was a Colorado schoolgirl when she designed the experiment she's known for, at nine, for a fourth-grade science fair. *Time* reported that the idea was hers. By the time the results were published in *JAMA* in 1998, she was 11.[^1][^2]

## Her contribution to skepticism

Therapeutic Touch (TT) is a nursing practice whose practitioners say they can feel and adjust a "human energy field" above a patient's skin. Its guides describe steps that don't actually require touching the patient.[^1] Rosa saw that this made a simple question possible: can practitioners feel the field at all?

Her test needed no lab. A practitioner rested both hands palms up and put them through a tall, opaque screen, with a towel draped over them. Rosa flipped a coin, held her hand 8 to 10 cm above one of theirs, and asked which hand it was.[^1] Chance alone would get about half right. The authors argued that someone who could really feel the field should get them all.[^1]

Before the test, every participant said TT worked, and all but one expressed high confidence in their abilities. Across two rounds of testing in 1996 and 1997, 21 practitioners with 1 to 27 years of experience picked the right hand in 123 of 280 tries, or 44%. Experience made no significant difference.[^1] The one person who reached the pass mark of 8 out of 10 got 6 on a retest.[^1]

What happened next is the most useful part for anyone learning to reason about claims. After the first round, some practitioners said their hands had grown too hot to sense anything. But TT sessions typically run 20 to 30 minutes, and the authors pointed out that if practitioners lose the ability after brief testing, "the TT hypothesis is untestable."[^1] A claim that gets a new exception after every failure can't be tested. That's what falsifiability is about.

The authors also suspected that the practitioners agreed to take part because the tester was a child "who displayed no skepticism."[^1] It's a good reminder that a test doesn't have to be complicated, as long as it's fair and the claim is clear.

## Mistakes and criticism

Rosa was a child, and most of the criticism is aimed at the published paper, not at her. The paper says she designed and ran the tests and tabulated the findings, while the adults did the rest: the recruiting, statistics, literature review and writing.[^1]

Those adults weren't neutral. Her mother, Linda Rosa, represented a task force on questionable nursing practices at the National Council Against Health Fraud. Larry Sarner was with the National Therapeutic Touch Study Group, and Stephen Barrett was with Quackwatch.[^1] The paper disclosed these ties, and a coin flip limits how much bias can affect a score, but readers were right to want to know.

There's also a fair question about consent. The first participants agreed to a project that would be published as a fourth-grade science fair entry. The decision to send it to a journal came several months later, and the paper doesn't say whether they were asked again.[^1]

The strongest objection is about how far the results reach. The paper tested one thing, whether practitioners could detect a hand, in a small group from northeastern Colorado. It then concluded that TT's claims are "groundless" and that further professional use is "unjustified."[^1] A failed test of TT's central claim is serious evidence, but one small study didn't measure whether patients improved.

TT's defenders pushed back hard, though they aren't neutral either. Its founder, Dolores Krieger, said, "It's a cute idea, but it's not valid."[^2] *JAMA* printed a run of letters on the paper, and the authors replied.[^3] In 2003, the nurse-statistician Thomas Cox published a reanalysis in an alternative medicine journal. He argued that the data contradicted the authors' conclusions, and he challenged their assumptions about whether the test could validate an existing skill.[^4] In 2011, former *JAMA* editor George Lundberg wrote that the study's science had stood the test of time for 13 years, while acknowledging that "there was not a uniform reader response."[^5]
