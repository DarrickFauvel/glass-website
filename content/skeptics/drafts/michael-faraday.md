## Who he was

Michael Faraday was born in Southwark, London, in 1791, the son of a blacksmith, and trained as a bookbinder from 1805 to 1812 before joining the Royal Institution as a laboratory assistant. He went on to direct its laboratory, and in the 1820s he founded the Friday Evening Discourses and Christmas Lectures that brought science to the public.[^1] In August 1831 he discovered electromagnetic induction, and within months he had built the first electric generator.[^2]

## The test

In table-turning, sitters rest their hands on a table and it seems to move by itself. In 1853, so many inquiries about it reached Faraday that he decided to state his views publicly, "to stop the inpouring flood."[^3]

He suspected what he called "a quasi involuntary muscular action," and he set out to determine "whether the table moved the hand, or the hand moved the table."[^4] To find out, he put a light lever on the table with a long arm that worked as an indicator. It would show which way the sitters' hands were really moving.

The result didn't depend on anyone's honesty. When the indicator was hidden, it wavered, "though they believed that they always pressed directly downwards." When sitters could watch it, "all effects of table-turning cease," even when they were "earnestly desiring motion."[^4]

Faraday's explanation is the part that has lasted. He didn't call the sitters liars. He said that seeing the indicator made them "unable unwittingly to deceive themselves."[^4] Sincere people can be wrong about what their own hands are doing, so a good test measures the hands.

## Mistakes and criticism

Faraday's test answered a narrow question, and it didn't end the belief. Months later, a correspondent wrote to him that the subject was still "much vexed," despite the letter to *The Times* "which every one has read," and described "most curious and startling facts" at his own home.[^5]

His private reaction was less patient than his public one. "What a weak credulous incredulous, unbelieving superstitious, bold, frightened, what a ridiculous world ours is," he wrote to the chemist Christian Schoenbein that July.[^3] The frustration is understandable, but it's also a reminder that showing how a trick of the mind works isn't the same as persuading people, and scorn rarely helps.
