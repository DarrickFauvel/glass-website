## Who he was

James Randi was born Randall James Hamilton Zwinge in Toronto in 1928.[^1] He left home as a teenager to work in a circus as a "mind reader" and escape artist, and went on to a long career as a stage magician.[^2] He died in 2020, at 92.[^1]

## His contribution to skepticism

Randi's training was his advantage. Scientists are used to studying nature, which doesn't try to trick them. A magician knows how people get fooled.

- **Faith healing.** He showed that the messages faith healer Peter Popoff claimed to receive from God were coming from his wife, through an earpiece.[^1]
- **Spoon bending.** He helped Johnny Carson set up a 1972 *Tonight Show* appearance by Uri Geller, who failed to perform his tricks.[^1]
- **The "memory of water."** In 1988, *Nature* sent him with its editor and a misconduct specialist to observe repeat experiments on a claim that seemed to support homeopathy.[^2]
- **The prize.** He offered money for decades to anyone who could demonstrate a paranormal power. His foundation raised the sum to $1 million in 1996 and ended the prize in 2015. Nobody collected it.[^1][^2]

The prize puts the burden of proof where it belongs: on the person making the claim. You can read more about it in our post on [whether psychics can really read minds](/blog/can-psychics-really-read-minds).

He also co-founded what is now the Committee for Skeptical Inquiry, in 1976.[^2]

## Mistakes and criticism

Randi's most disputed work is Project Alpha. He sent two young magicians, Mike Edwards and Steve Shaw, to the McDonnell Laboratory for Psychical Research at Washington University, posing as genuine metal-benders and using trickery. He revealed the hoax at a media event in January 1983, and said it was a scientific experiment to test how well the lab could cope with magicians.[^3]

The hoax did show that the researchers had been fooled at first. Martin Gardner, a fellow skeptic, praised it as a "landmark hoax."[^3]

The criticism comes mainly from the lab's side, so it isn't neutral, but it deserves a hearing. The lab's director, Peter Phillips, says the staff took Randi's advice in 1981, ran fraud-free experiments, saw no psychic effects and stopped working with the two subjects in 1982, and that much of the later coverage left this out. He also objects to the method: "When lies and deception are used, outside a stage setting, to deceive honest people, subject them to public ridicule and damage their reputations, one might think there was some explaining to do."[^3]

Both things can be true. The hoax exposed a real weakness in the early work, and the lab then corrected it, which is how science is supposed to work. Whether the deception was justified is still a fair question.
