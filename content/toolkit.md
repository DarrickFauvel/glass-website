Skepticism isn't cynicism, and it isn't about winning arguments. It's a set of habits for working out what's likely to be true, especially when we'd *like* something to be true. The tools below are the ones that come up most often in our [blog posts](/blog) and meetups. Each one has a short definition, an example from the kinds of claims we look at, and a source if you want to go deeper.

One rule before you start: these tools are for checking *our own* thinking first. Spotting a fallacy in someone else's argument is easy. Catching one in your own takes practice.

## Carl Sagan's Baloney Detection Kit

In *The Demon-Haunted World* (1995), astronomer Carl Sagan described a "baloney detection kit," a set of tools to use "whenever new ideas are offered for consideration." Here are his nine, lightly paraphrased:[^1]

1. **Get independent confirmation of the "facts."** One source isn't enough.
2. **Encourage real debate** on the evidence by knowledgeable people from all points of view.
3. **Arguments from authority carry little weight.** Authorities have made mistakes before and will again. In Sagan's words, "in science there are no authorities; at most, there are experts."
4. **Spin more than one hypothesis.** Think of every way something could be explained, then look for tests that could rule each one out.
5. **Don't get overly attached to a hypothesis just because it's yours.** Ask why you like it, and look for reasons to reject it. If you don't, others will.
6. **Quantify.** Numbers let you tell competing explanations apart. Vague claims fit almost anything.
7. **Every link in a chain of argument must work,** including the premise, not just most of them.
8. **Occam's razor.** When two hypotheses explain the data equally well, choose the simpler one.
9. **Ask whether the hypothesis can, at least in principle, be falsified.** Claims that can't be tested "are not worth much."

Sagan paired the kit with a list of common reasoning errors to avoid, many of which appear in the fallacies section below.[^1]

## Reasoning tools

### Occam's razor

The classic statement is "entities are not to be multiplied beyond necessity": don't assume more than you need to explain what you see.[^2] When a light in the sky can be explained by a weather balloon *or* by a balloon-shaped alien craft plus a decades-long cover-up, the razor favors the balloon.

It's a rule of thumb, not a law. Philosophers still debate exactly why simplicity should track truth, and a simple theory can still be wrong.[^2] The razor tells you where to *start*, not where you must end up.

### Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence

The more a claim conflicts with everything else we know, the stronger the evidence it needs. The idea is old. NASA's UAP study team traced it back to an 1808 letter by Thomas Jefferson, who wrote that such claims need "proofs proportioned to their difficulty."[^3] A friend saying they saw a deer on the drive home needs no special proof. A friend saying they saw a nine-foot ape needs a lot more than their word. That isn't because we think they're lying. It's because honest people can be mistaken, and the claim is extraordinary.

### The burden of proof

Whoever makes a claim has to support it. It's a fallacy to insist that someone else prove you *wrong* when it's your job to show you're right.[^4]

> "You can't prove psychics aren't real."

That isn't evidence that they are.

### Falsifiability

A useful claim says what would count *against* it.[^1]

> "This medium talks to the dead, but the spirits go quiet whenever a skeptic is in the room."

That claim can't fail, because every failure gets explained away. A claim that can't fail can't really be tested, so it can't tell us anything either.

### Base rates: "compared to what?"

Before you're impressed by a hit, ask how often you'd get that hit by chance. People tend to ignore these background frequencies, or *base rates*, when a specific story feels convincing.[^5] A reading that mentions "a family member with a heart problem" sounds specific until you ask how many families *don't* have one.

**In action:** [Can Psychics Really Read Minds?](/blog/can-psychics-really-read-minds)

### Absence of evidence, when evidence is expected

"Absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence" is only half right. If nobody has looked, finding nothing means little. But if a claim *predicts* evidence, such as bones, bodies, or DNA, and people have searched hard for decades without finding any, the absence starts to count.

**In action:** [Is Bigfoot Out There?](/blog/is-bigfoot-out-there)

### The body of evidence beats a single study

Any one study can be wrong, through chance, a flaw in its design, or something nobody has spotted yet. The National Academies of Sciences note that reviewing the *cumulative* evidence on a question is often a more useful guide than any single result. They also note that a failed replication doesn't always mean someone did something wrong.[^6] When a striking new finding contradicts a large body of earlier work, wait to see whether it replicates.

## Logical fallacies

A *fallacy* is a flaw in reasoning: an argument whose conclusion doesn't actually follow from its reasons. The definitions below follow the *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*'s guide to fallacies.[^4]

Think you can spot them? [Test yourself with Spot the Fallacy](/toolkit/quiz), a quick quiz of ten short cases.

### Ad hominem

Attacking the person making an argument instead of the argument itself.[^4]

> "Of course he says vaccines are safe, he's a doctor."

That dismisses the speaker without addressing the evidence. The same goes in reverse: calling believers "idiots" refutes nothing.

### Straw man

Attributing an easily refuted position to your opponent instead of the one they actually hold.[^4]

> "Skeptics think everyone who's seen a UFO is lying."

Most skeptics think witnesses saw something real and misidentified it.

### Appeal to authority

Backing up a claim mainly by pointing to someone who supports it.[^4] Expertise matters, but it's evidence, not proof, and it only counts within the expert's own field. A celebrated physicist endorsing a supplement is speaking outside theirs.

### Appeal to ignorance

Treating a lack of proof as proof of the opposite.[^4]

> "Nobody has explained that video, so it must be an alien craft."

That fills a gap in knowledge with a conclusion.

### Post hoc

Short for *post hoc, ergo propter hoc*, Latin for "after this, therefore because of this": jumping to the conclusion that because B came after A, A caused B.[^4]

> "I wore a copper bracelet and my arthritis got better."

That ignores that many conditions improve on their own. Most people with a cold, for example, feel better after a week or two, and there's no cure.[^13]

### False dilemma

Presenting too few options and implying you must choose one of them.[^4]

> "Either contrails are normal condensation or the government is spraying us."

That leaves out everything in between, and skips the evidence that settles it.

### Slippery slope

Arguing that one step will inevitably lead to an extreme outcome, while exaggerating how likely that chain really is.[^4]

> "First they put fluoride in the water. Next it'll be sedatives, and after that, whatever keeps us quiet."

That skips every step in between, and each one would need its own evidence. Some chains of consequences are real, so the useful question is how likely each link actually is.

### Special pleading

Applying a standard to everyone else but exempting your own case.[^4] A classic example:

> "Controlled tests don't work on psychic powers because the skeptical atmosphere blocks them."

### Cherry-picking

Leaving out evidence you suspect is relevant because it doesn't fit; the *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy* calls this the fallacy of suppressed evidence.[^4] Quoting the one study that found harm while ignoring the hundreds that didn't is cherry-picking.

### Bandwagon

Suggesting a claim is true because most people believe it, or more and more people are coming to.[^4] Popularity tells you what people think, not what's true.

A crowd can even grow with no new evidence at all. In an *informational cascade*, each person does best, from where they stand, by following the people ahead of them and setting aside what they know themselves.[^14]

### Anecdotal evidence

Generalizing from a story that's too small a sample to support the conclusion.[^4]

That's a real experience. It just can't tell you whether the treatment works in general.

## Cognitive biases

Fallacies are errors in arguments. *Cognitive biases* are predictable quirks in how all of us notice, remember, and weigh information. They aren't signs of stupidity. They're shortcuts that usually serve us well and occasionally lead us astray.[^5]

### The Dunning–Kruger effect

In a famous 1999 study, people who scored lowest on tests of logic, grammar, and humor overestimated their performance the most. They scored around the 12th percentile but guessed they were around the 62nd.[^7] The authors argued that the skills needed to do something well are often the same skills needed to recognize doing it badly.

**A skeptic's footnote on this one:** the effect is often exaggerated in popular culture. Later researchers argued that much of the classic pattern can come from statistical artifacts, such as most people rating themselves above average. Using different methods, they found the effect may be much smaller than first reported.[^8] It's a useful reminder to be humble about what you know. It's not a scientific license to call other people incompetent.

### Confirmation bias

Seeking out or interpreting evidence in ways that favor what you already believe.[^9] It's why a believer remembers the one dream that "came true" and forgets the hundreds that didn't. Skeptics are just as prone to it, which is why we lean on controls and blind testing rather than our own impressions.

### Motivated reasoning

When we *want* a particular conclusion, we tend to reach it, as long as we can build a reasonable-sounding case for it.[^10] It's why the question "do I want this to be true?" is worth asking about any claim, in either direction.

### The availability heuristic

Judging how common or likely something is by how easily examples come to mind.[^5] Vivid stories, like a dramatic plane crash or a haunting on TV, feel more common than they are.

### The Barnum effect

Accepting vague, general personality descriptions as uniquely true of you.[^11] It's the engine of horoscopes and of the "cold reading" behind many psychic performances.

### Pareidolia

Seeing meaningful patterns, especially faces, in random noise. In one experiment, people who were told some pure-noise images contained faces reported seeing faces about a third of the time.[^12] It's why we see faces in clouds, toast, and grainy photos of the woods.

## A final tool: the fallacy fallacy

A bad argument doesn't make its conclusion false. Someone can defend a true claim badly, with an ad hominem or a cherry-picked study, and the claim can still be true. These tools help you judge the *reasoning*. To judge the claim itself, you still have to go and look at the evidence.
