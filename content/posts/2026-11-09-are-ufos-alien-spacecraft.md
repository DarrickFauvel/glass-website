## The claim

Some of the objects people see in the sky, the claim goes, are spacecraft built by intelligences from somewhere else. In its strongest form, the claim adds that governments know this. Craft, and maybe bodies, have been recovered and studied in secret, and the public is being kept in the dark.

Governments now use a newer term, *unidentified anomalous phenomena* (UAP), for anything in the air, sea, or space that observers can't immediately explain. The change of name matters, because it separates two very different statements. "We saw something we couldn't identify" is a report. "We saw an alien spacecraft" is a conclusion.

## Why it's compelling

This isn't a belief held only by a few enthusiasts. In a July 2026 Gallup poll, 37% of Americans said at least some UFOs have been alien spacecraft. 78% said the U.S. government knows more about UFOs than it is telling the public.[^1]

And the sightings aren't just lights seen by tired drivers. In 2021, the U.S. intelligence community reviewed 144 reports, mostly from military aviators, collected between 2004 and 2021. It could confidently identify only one of them. In 18 incidents, observers described objects that seemed to hover in high winds, move against the wind, or maneuver abruptly "without discernable means of propulsion."[^2] When trained pilots report something like that, and the government publicly says it can't explain it, it's natural to wonder.

## What the evidence shows

**"Unexplained" usually means "not enough data."** The same 2021 assessment explained why it identified so little. Military sensors are built for other missions, not for identifying small unknown objects, and the reports often lacked the needed detail. The assessment warned that the strange flight behavior "could be the result of sensor errors, spoofing, or observer misperception." It expected that resolved cases would fall into ordinary bins: airborne clutter such as birds and balloons, natural atmospheric effects, secret U.S. programs, foreign adversary systems, and "other."[^2]

**When more data comes in, most cases turn ordinary.** By August 2022 the catalog had grown to 510 reports. Of 366 new reports, the government's newly created UAP office judged 163 to be balloons or balloon-like, 26 to be drones or drone-like, and 6 to be clutter. That left 171 uncharacterized, and the report stressed that these still lacked the data needed for a firm answer.[^3]

**The famous videos look different once you do the math.** One of the best-known Navy clips, "GoFast," seems to show an object racing just above the ocean. NASA's independent UAP study team worked through the numbers on the targeting display: the camera angle, the range, and the jet's own altitude. They found the object was about 13,000 feet up, not skimming the waves. Its apparent speed came mostly from the jet's own motion and parallax, the way nearby objects seem to fly past a car window. The object's actual speed was roughly 40 mph, typical wind speed at that height. It was also colder than the ocean behind it, so it showed no sign of an engine.[^4]

**Sightings follow opportunity.** A 2023 study mapped more than 98,000 public UAP reports across the continental United States from 2001 to 2020. It looked at how much sky people can actually see (light pollution, tree cover, clouds) and how much is up there to see (aircraft traffic, military installations). The pattern was clear: people report more "phenomena" when they have more opportunity to see things.[^5] That's what you'd expect if most reports are ordinary objects seen under ordinary conditions.

**The cover-up story hasn't held up.** In 2024, the Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) reviewed U.S. government UAP investigations from 1945 onward and interviewed people who had claimed knowledge of hidden programs. It found no evidence that any official investigation had ever confirmed a UAP was extraterrestrial technology, and "no empirical evidence" that the government or private companies had been reverse-engineering alien craft. AARO traced the modern version of the claim largely to "circular reporting" among a small, consistent group of people repeating each other's accounts. It also noted that some real sightings over the decades were of secret aircraft and rockets, including stealth technology. So the government really did keep secrets about some of those sightings. It just wasn't hiding aliens.[^6]

## The question

> "We don't know what this is" is a legitimate scientific position — but what specific evidence distinguishes an unexplained atmospheric phenomenon from an alien spacecraft?

The first half of that question is right, and it's worth defending. Some cases remain unexplained, and saying so honestly is good science. But an unexplained case isn't evidence *for* any particular explanation. It's a gap in the data.

Evidence that would actually point to alien technology looks different. It would mean physical material that independent labs could examine and that couldn't have been made on Earth. It would mean measurements from several well-calibrated instruments that agree with one another and rule out balloons, drones, birds, and glare. And it would mean results that hold up when other scientists check them. NASA's study team put it plainly: in the peer-reviewed scientific literature, "there is no conclusive evidence suggesting an extraterrestrial origin for UAP." The team called extraterrestrial life the "hypothesis of last resort," the answer to turn to only once every other possibility has been ruled out.[^4]

None of this proves we're alone, and it doesn't mean pilots are making things up. They're reporting real objects they couldn't identify, often for good reasons. It just means that, so far, every case with enough data to solve has had an earthly answer.[^3][^6] (For how to read the videos and documents the Pentagon began releasing in 2026, see [Do the Pentagon's UFO Files Show Aliens?](/blog/do-the-pentagons-ufo-files-show-aliens).)

## The thinking tool: "unidentified" isn't an identification

When something can't be explained, it's tempting to fill the gap with the most exciting answer. Philosophers call this an *argument from ignorance*: "we can't explain it, so it must be X."

The honest move is to let "unknown" stay unknown until the evidence names something specific. A blurry shape is consistent with a balloon, a drone, a bird, a reflection, *or* a spacecraft, so it can't count as evidence for just one of them. Ask what each explanation predicts that the others don't, then go and look for that.
