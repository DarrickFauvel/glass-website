## The claim

Prayer is one of the most common things people do. In a 2023–24 survey, 44% of US adults said they pray every day.[^1] When someone is seriously ill, many people's first response is to pray for them.

This post looks at one specific claim: praying for a sick person makes that person more likely to get better, even if they don't know about it. Researchers call this *intercessory prayer*.

That claim needs separating from two other questions, because they often get bundled together:

1. **Does praying for someone else change their health?** This can be tested by comparing groups of patients.
2. **Does praying help the person who prays?** This is a different question, and we'll come back to it.
3. **Does God exist, and does God answer prayer?** These studies can't settle that. The largest US trial said plainly that it wasn't designed to determine this, and the leading review treats it as a philosophical question outside its scope.[^2][^3]

So nothing here is a verdict on anyone's faith. The question is narrower: can we measure a health benefit?

## Why it's compelling

The experience behind this belief is real and often profound. Someone you love is very sick, people pray, and they recover. The early research seemed to agree. In 1988, a randomized trial of 393 coronary care patients reported that those who received prayer had better hospital courses.[^4] A 1999 trial of 990 patients also found lower scores on a measure of complications, though the patients' hospital stays were no shorter.[^5]

Those were serious studies in medical journals, so it was reasonable to take them seriously and to test the idea further.

## What the evidence shows

The idea of testing prayer isn't new. In 1872, Francis Galton pointed out that people publicly prayed for the health of sovereigns, yet royals lived no longer than other wealthy people. His comparison was crude by modern standards, but the basic logic was sound: compare groups.[^6]

Modern trials do this properly, by assigning patients at random and keeping them and their doctors unaware of who is being prayed for.

- **MANTRA II (2005).** With 748 heart patients in nine US centers, off-site prayer by congregations of various religions didn't significantly improve outcomes.[^7]
- **STEP (2006).** The largest of these trials followed 1,802 bypass patients at six US hospitals. Among patients who didn't know whether they were receiving prayer, complications occurred in 52% of those who did and 51% of those who didn't.[^2]

STEP also had a surprise. Patients who were told for certain that they'd receive prayer had more complications (59%) than those who were unsure (52%).[^2] It would be a mistake to conclude that prayer is harmful. That's one unexpected result, and the study didn't show why it happened.

The pooled evidence points the same way. A 2006 meta-analysis of 14 studies found an overall effect that didn't differ from zero, and removing one controversial study shrank it almost to nothing.[^8] A Cochrane review of ten studies with 7,646 patients found no clear effect on death or on general clinical state, and it called the findings "equivocal." It says the evidence doesn't support a recommendation for or against intercessory prayer. It also questions whether more trials are worth the money.[^3]

Two cases show why a single dramatic result isn't enough:

- **The fertility study.** A 2001 paper reported that women in a South Korean IVF clinic who received prayer were twice as likely to conceive.[^9][^10] The man who set up and managed its prayer groups, Daniel Wirth, was later sentenced to prison for financial fraud unrelated to the study, and an author first described as its lead author said he had only reviewed and edited the report.[^10] That doesn't prove the data were false, but it does show why results need independent replication.
- **The retroactive study.** In a 2001 *BMJ* paper, a prayer was said in 2000 for a randomly chosen group of patients who had been treated in 1990–96. The paper reported shorter hospital stays and fevers in that group.[^11] Whatever its author intended, it makes the point well. If the same kind of statistics can "show" that a prayer changed the past, a low p-value alone can't establish a cause.

These trials do have limits. They can only test *additional* prayer, because nobody can stop families from praying for the patients in the comparison group. Some believers also object to the whole project. As Sharon Fish Mooney wrote in the *Christian Research Journal*, "God is not a genie, and prayer is not magic."[^12] That's a fair theological position, but it means that the claim can't be tested, and it doesn't support the opposite claim that studies have proved prayer works.

## The question

So can prayer heal the sick? Controlled studies haven't shown that praying for patients improves their health. That's not the same as proving that prayer can't ever affect anything. It means that, when researchers looked carefully, in thousands of patients, no clear benefit appeared.

The second question has a different answer. Even Galton wrote that "the mind may be relieved by the utterance of prayer."[^6] Researchers have studied prayer as a way of coping, but a review found that the scientific evidence on prayer and health is limited.[^13] Many people find comfort, community and meaning in prayer, and these trials don't take that away.

What matters most in practice is that prayer shouldn't replace medical care. The American Academy of Pediatrics says that when treatment is likely to prevent death or serious disability or to relieve severe pain, children's health should be protected, even if parents object on religious grounds.[^14] This post isn't medical advice, and anyone who is ill should talk to a doctor.

## The thinking tool

"I prayed, and she got better" is a sincere and moving thing to say, and many people have said it. But it's also a case of *post hoc* reasoning: because B came after A, A must have caused B.

Most people recover from many illnesses anyway, and we tend to remember the recoveries more vividly than the times prayer went unanswered. One story can't tell these possibilities apart. That's why a fair test compares similar people, with and without the thing being tested. The same goes for vitamins, new drugs, and anything else we'd like to believe works.
