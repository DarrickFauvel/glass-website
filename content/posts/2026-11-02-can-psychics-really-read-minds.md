## The claim

Some people, the claim goes, can perceive things the rest of us can't: what a stranger is thinking, what will happen next week, or what a departed relative wants to say. In its strongest form, this is a claim about information. A psychic learns true, specific things through a channel other than the five senses.

That's a bold claim, but it's also a testable one. If information is getting through, we should be able to catch it getting through when every ordinary route is blocked.

## Why it's compelling

Belief in psychics isn't fringe. A 2018 Pew Research Center survey found that about four in ten American adults believe in psychics.[^1]

Much of that belief comes from experience rather than theory. People sit down with a reader, hear things that feel too accurate to be guesswork, and leave shaken. Those sessions really do feel remarkable, and the people who come away convinced are not foolish. Psychologists who study the belief describe ordinary mental habits behind it: judging probabilities by feel, and spotting connections between loosely related things. Those habits make it easy to give a paranormal explanation to an experience that has a normal one.[^2]

## What the evidence shows

**A reading can feel accurate without any psychic input.** Researchers call the technique *cold reading*: a reader makes calculated guesses, watches how the client reacts, and steers toward whatever lands. It leans heavily on the *Barnum effect*, our tendency to accept a vague, general personality sketch as a description of ourselves in particular.[^3] Statements like "you can be very social, but you also need time alone" fit nearly everyone. The client remembers the hits, forgets the misses, and often supplies the specifics without noticing.

**When mediums are tested with the cues removed, the effect disappears.** Psychologists Ciarán O'Keeffe and Richard Wiseman designed a test of professional mediums that blocked the usual sources of leakage. Mediums and sitters were kept in separate rooms, and each sitter rated statements from every reading without knowing which reading had been meant for them. If the mediums were picking up something real, sitters should have rated their own readings as the best fit. In most cases they didn't. The authors concluded that the results "did not support the existence of genuine mediumistic ability."[^4][^11] This is the key move in any good test. If the sitter can't tell their own reading from someone else's, the reading wasn't about them.

**Lab results shrink as the controls tighten.** The most-studied telepathy setup is the *ganzfeld*, a mild sensory-deprivation procedure in which a "receiver" tries to pick out an image that a "sender" in another room is looking at. Early studies looked promising. But a 1999 meta-analysis (a study that pools the results of many studies) of 30 newer ganzfeld experiments from seven laboratories found an overall effect indistinguishable from chance. The authors concluded that the method "does not at present offer a replicable method for producing ESP in the laboratory."[^5] Parapsychologists answered with a larger pooled analysis that did find a small positive effect.[^6] The disagreement is real. It's mostly about which studies count and how well each one was controlled, and that pattern is itself telling.

**Splashy findings haven't replicated.** In 2011, psychologist Daryl Bem published nine experiments in a leading journal suggesting that people could "feel the future," for example by recalling words better if they would study them *later*.[^7] It caused a stir. A team of psychologists then ran seven careful replications with 3,289 participants. They found nothing, and when they pooled every replication attempt, the average effect was "no different from 0."[^8]

**The U.S. government tried it, too.** For years, U.S. intelligence agencies funded "remote viewing," the claimed ability to describe distant places by mind alone. A 1995 outside evaluation, with a review panel that included one statistician sympathetic to the research and one skeptic, reported that lab hits did occur more often than chance. But it couldn't attribute them to psychic ability rather than quirks of the judges, targets, or methods. In the field, the reports were vague and "required substantial subjective interpretation." No information from them had ever been used to guide an intelligence operation.[^9]

## The question

> Why do psychic powers vanish when double-blind testing rules out cold reading and confirmation bias?

The simplest answer is that the powers were never doing the work. The impressive part of a reading comes from the reader's skill and the client's generous memory, both of which double-blind testing removes on purpose.[^3][^4] What's left in the lab is a scattering of small effects. They tend to fade as methods tighten and haven't held up when independent teams try to repeat them.[^5][^8]

The hero clue mentions the James Randi Educational Foundation's $1 million prize, which no one ever won. Randi designed those tests so that "the results are self-evident," with success criteria agreed in advance.[^10] That is a striking fact, but a prize isn't a scientific study. Plenty of sincere believers never apply. The stronger evidence is the research record above, built by scientists on both sides of the question.

None of this means psychics are all deliberate frauds. Many sincerely believe in their gift. One review of the cold-reading research even raises the question of how many readers have convinced themselves.[^3]

## The thinking tool: ask "compared to what?"

A hit only means something if you know how often you'd get one by chance. "Someone in your family had a problem with their chest" sounds specific, until you ask how many families *don't* have someone like that.

That's why good tests use controls: a decoy reading, a target the sender didn't choose, or a judge who doesn't know which answer is right. Whenever a result sounds amazing, ask what the same method would produce if nothing unusual were going on. If the answer is "about the same," you've learned something important, and it isn't about psychic powers.
