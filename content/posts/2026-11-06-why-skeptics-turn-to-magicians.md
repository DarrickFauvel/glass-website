## The claim

When someone claims a paranormal power, it's natural to think that the best judge is a trained scientist. In its strongest form, the idea is this: a careful researcher with good equipment, who watches closely and takes notes, is hard to fool. So if that researcher saw something they can't explain, we should take it seriously.

## Why it's compelling

There's a lot of truth in it. Scientists do have training in measurement, statistics and controls. Those skills are exactly what you'd want for testing most claims.

But a paranormal claim isn't like most claims, because the thing being studied might be a person who wants to fool you. A planet doesn't cheat. As the writer Matthew Tompkins has put it, scientists "are not necessarily trained in deception."[^1]

Magicians are. Over centuries, they've learned to make things seem to defy the laws of nature, and psychologists now describe that craft in three main areas: controlling attention, distorting perception and influencing choice.[^2]

## What the evidence shows

Magicians and researchers have been working on this for a long time, and some of what they've found applies to all of us.

**We misremember what we saw.** In the 1880s, the magician S. J. Davey learned to fake séance effects and invited small groups to watch, working with the psychical researcher Richard Hodgson. Many sitters forgot or misremembered key details. In one trick, a slate was removed and later replaced, and that was the secret of the effect. People later reported that it had stayed in place until ghostly writing appeared on it. This work came more than a decade before similar research in mainstream psychology.[^3]

**We see what we expect.** In the "vanishing ball" trick, a magician throws a ball into the air twice, then pretends to throw it a third time. In one study, 68% of 38 viewers saw a ball that was never thrown. Viewers said they had kept their eyes on the ball, but their eyes reached the top of the screen only when a real ball was thrown.[^4] When the magician looked at his hand instead of up, only 32% saw the illusion, which suggested that his gaze was what sold it.[^4][^5] A later study challenged that explanation: almost two-thirds of viewers still saw the ball when the magician wore a mask.[^6] Researchers disagree about why it works, but in both studies many people saw something that didn't happen.

**We can be told what we saw.** In a staged séance, an actor said that a table was levitating, when it never moved. About a third of participants later reported that it had moved. Believers in the paranormal were more likely to say so, but only when the suggestion fit their beliefs.[^7] In another study, a fake psychic put a bent key on a table and said it was still bending. People who heard this were more likely to report that it had kept bending, and those who did were more confident in their testimony. In that study, belief made no difference, so doubters were fooled just as often.[^8]

**Trained researchers can be fooled too.** [Harry Houdini](/skeptics/harry-houdini) sat on a *Scientific American* committee in the 1920s because a magician could spot methods that others missed. Decades later, [James Randi](/skeptics/james-randi) sent two young magicians to a psychic research lab as fake metal-benders. The lab's early work was fooled, though its director says the staff later tightened their controls, saw no psychic effects and dropped the pair before the hoax was revealed.[^9] That's worth remembering: good controls did the job in the end.

The exchange runs both ways now. In 2008, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* published a paper on what stage magic can teach researchers about attention and awareness, and its authors included Randi and Teller of Penn & Teller.[^10] Magic tricks have become a research tool.

## The question

So why do skeptics keep turning to magicians? Because they ask a different question. A scientist tends to ask, "What could explain this result?" A magician asks, "How would I fake it?" If a paranormal claim might involve trickery, you need someone who can think of the trick.

That doesn't make magicians the final word, and it's worth being careful here:

- **Showing that a trick is possible isn't proof that a trick was used.** It only means that the demonstration can't count as evidence of a power until trickery is ruled out.
- **Magicians can fool themselves too.** Nothing in this research shows that they're immune to the errors of memory and attention that they use on others.
- **Most mediums and psychics aren't shown to be frauds by this.** Someone can sincerely believe in their own abilities. A tightly controlled test doesn't need anyone to be lying.

The best test uses both skills. A magician helps close off the ways of cheating, and a scientist designs the test and measures the results. The aim isn't to catch anyone out, but to find out what's true, however it turns out.

## The thinking tool

This is a good case for asking, "Expert in what?" An appeal to authority is only as strong as the match between the person's expertise and the question. A brilliant physicist is an expert on physics. They aren't automatically an expert on whether someone is cheating at a table in front of them, and neither are the rest of us, however sure we feel about what we saw.

So when you hear "a scientist saw it" or "I saw it with my own eyes," ask what would have to be true for that to count as evidence, and who in the room would have noticed if it wasn't.
