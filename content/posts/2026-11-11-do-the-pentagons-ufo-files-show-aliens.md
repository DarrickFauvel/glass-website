## The claim

On May 8, 2026, the Pentagon began posting declassified UFO files online. The first batch held 162 files, and the Pentagon said more would follow "on a rolling basis."[^1] More did follow. By October it had published a seventh batch,[^2] adding videos, photos, audio recordings and documents along the way.[^6]

For many people watching, the conclusion seems obvious. The government wouldn't release these unless something extraordinary was in them. If the military's own cameras recorded objects it can't explain, and it's now showing them to us, that looks like a slow, official confirmation that we're being visited.

## Why it's compelling

The source is about as official as it gets. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said the files "have long fueled justified speculation."[^3] The Pentagon called one 2023 report of orbs "among the most compelling" it had received.[^4] Its own announcement said the public "can ultimately make up their own minds about the information contained in these files."[^1]

The material looks serious, too. It includes new videos and reports from pilots.[^5] Some files also have entire pages blacked out,[^4] which naturally makes people wonder what's under the black bars.

## What the evidence shows

**Released isn't the same as explained.** Declassifying a file makes it public. It doesn't mean anyone has concluded anything about it. Sean Kirkpatrick, a former director of the Pentagon's UFO office, said there was "nothing unexpected" in the first release.[^5] He warned that "without any analysis or context," it would mostly fuel speculation.[^4] When a file does include analysis, the analysis is often uncertain. The third batch included an intelligence partner's review of a 2022 incident in Colorado Springs. It offered "sunlight backscattering" as an explanation but rated that explanation "low confidence," and CBS News reported that the case remained unresolved.[^6] An unresolved case is one where the evidence ran out, not one where it pointed to aliens.

**Not all of it is new.** The seventh batch included a 2002 statement by Walter Haut, the public information officer at Roswell Army Air Field in 1947. In it, Haut described seeing an egg-shaped craft and two small bodies in a hangar. NewsNation reported that its content had already been published in a 2007 book.[^2] The third batch included a CIA panel report from 1952–1953.[^6] Posting an old document on a government website gives it a new address, not new evidence. Haut's account says what it said in 2007, and it's still one person's testimony.

**A video without its context can't be analyzed.** NASA's independent study team on UFOs, which uses the government term *unidentified anomalous phenomena* (UAP), was blunt about the data. It wrote that analysis "is hampered by poor sensor calibration, the lack of multiple measurements, the lack of sensor metadata, and the lack of baseline data."[^7] Metadata is the information recorded alongside an image, "such as the time, location, and sensor observing modes." Without it, you can't tell how far away something is or how the camera was moving, so you can't tell how big or fast the object is. The team also noted that "several apparent UAP have been demonstrated to be sensor artifacts once appropriate calibration and metadata scrutiny were applied."[^7]

The Navy's "GoFast" video shows what context can do. It seems to show an object racing over the ocean. Using the numbers on the targeting display, NASA's team calculated that the object was about 13,000 feet up, moving at about 40 mph, a typical wind speed at that height. Much of its apparent speed came from the jet's own motion and parallax, the way nearby things seem to rush past a car window.[^7] (Our post [Are UFOs Alien Spacecraft?](/blog/are-ufos-alien-spacecraft) walks through it.) Independent researcher Mick West, reviewing the first batch, found "nothing really interesting" so far and summed it up in four words: "More dots, more parallax."[^5]

**When there's enough data, the answers are ordinary.** The Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) reports what happens to the cases it receives. Its 2024 annual report covered 757 reports. By the time it was published, 292 had been resolved, and every one turned out to be an ordinary object: balloons, birds, drones, satellites or aircraft. Another 444 cases "lacked sufficient data to facilitate analysis" and were archived. AARO flagged 21 as needing further study. It added that "to date, AARO has discovered no evidence of extraterrestrial beings, activity, or technology."[^8]

## The question

> If a government UFO video is going to count as evidence of something extraordinary, what does it need to contain?

It needs context. That means calibrated sensors, metadata giving the time, location, range and camera settings, and ideally several independent instruments that agree.[^7] It also needs published analysis that other people can check. A clip that stays unexplained after all of that would be genuinely interesting. A clip that's unexplained because nobody knows how far away the object was is just a clip with missing information.

This doesn't mean the releases are worthless, or that the people who filmed them were mistaken about seeing something. Making government records public is good, and skeptics should welcome it. AARO itself says some cases merit further study,[^8] and "we don't know yet" is an honest answer for those. The mistake is treating the release as a verdict. A file marked "unresolved" is a question, not an answer.

## The thinking tool: "compared to what?"

A collection of unexplained cases is, almost by definition, a collection chosen because the cases are unexplained. Looking only at that pile tells you nothing about how often "unexplained" turns into "alien." For that, you need the background numbers, called *base rates*: out of all the reports, how many were solved, and what did they turn out to be?

For UFOs, the government has published that number. Every case AARO had enough data to resolve in its 2024 report turned out to be something ordinary.[^8] So when a new video seems baffling, the first question isn't "what else could it be?" It's "what did the last few hundred like it turn out to be?"
