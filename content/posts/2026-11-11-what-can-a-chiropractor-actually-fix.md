## The claim

Chiropractic was started by D. D. Palmer, a magnetic healer. He proposed that small misalignments of the spine, which he called *vertebral subluxations*, interfere with the body's ability to heal itself.[^1] On that theory, adjusting the spine doesn't only ease a sore back. It restores health more generally, and regular adjustments help keep you well.

Today, most people who see a chiropractor go for pain. In 2022, 85.7% of U.S. adults who used chiropractic care said it was for pain management.[^2] But the strong version of the claim is still around: that a chiropractor can treat a broad range of health problems, from headaches to children's ear infections, by correcting the spine.

## Why it's compelling

Lots of people go, and lots of them feel better. In a 2017 U.S. government survey, 10.3% of adults had seen a chiropractor in the past year, up from 9.1% in 2012.[^3] Back pain is common, it hurts, and it can be frightening. A chiropractor offers hands-on care for the sore spot and a treatment that doesn't involve pills.[^4]

There's also a real kernel of truth. Spinal manipulation, the quick, controlled thrust to a spinal joint that chiropractors are known for, isn't nonsense. Medical guidelines include it as an option for back pain.[^5] So when someone says "my chiropractor fixed my back," they may well have had a treatment that helped.

## What the evidence shows

**For low back pain, it helps about as much as other recommended treatments.** The American College of Physicians lists spinal manipulation among its recommended nondrug options for low back pain, alongside heat, massage, acupuncture and exercise. It rates the evidence for manipulation as low quality.[^5] Reviews of the trials point the same way. For acute low back pain, a 2017 meta-analysis in *JAMA* found that manipulation was linked to "modest improvements in pain and function" for up to six weeks.[^6] For chronic low back pain, a 2019 review of 47 trials in *The BMJ* found that manipulation "produces similar effects to recommended therapies."[^7] That's a fair result for a treatment. It just isn't a special one.

**The technique isn't unique to chiropractors.** Most spinal manipulation is done by chiropractors, but osteopathic physicians and physical therapists use it too.[^4] So the useful question about back pain isn't really "chiropractor or doctor?" It's which of several modestly helpful treatments suits you, and who offers it.

**Beyond the back, the evidence mostly runs out.** A 2010 evidence report, published in a chiropractic journal, graded manual therapy condition by condition. It found manipulation effective for low back pain and some headaches. It also found the evidence inconclusive for children's ear infections and bedwetting. It found spinal manipulation "not effective" for asthma, painful periods or infant colic when compared with a sham (fake) manipulation.[^8]

**The founding theory hasn't held up.** A 2009 review in a chiropractic journal tested the subluxation idea against the standard epidemiological criteria for whether something causes disease. It concluded that "there is a significant lack of evidence to fulfill the basic criteria of causation."[^9] A 2006 review noted that much of the profession is still built on the subluxation theory.[^1] That gap is where most skeptical criticism lands. The problem isn't manipulation for back pain. It's selling spinal adjustment as a treatment for health in general.

**Side effects are common but usually mild. Serious harm is rare and debated.** In large case series, 50% to 67% of patients reported short-lived effects such as more pain, stiffness or headache after manipulation.[^6] Neck manipulation is the more serious concern. It has been linked to tears in the arteries of the neck, which can cause a stroke. An Ontario study of 818 such strokes found that younger patients were more likely to have seen a chiropractor shortly before their stroke. They were just as much more likely to have seen a family doctor. The authors concluded that people in the early stages of an artery tear, which causes neck pain and headache, were probably seeking care for those symptoms.[^10] The American Heart Association says it's unclear whether manipulation causes these tears. It advises that patients be told about the statistical link before neck manipulation.[^11]

**Is it a business?** Every kind of health care is partly a business. The fair question is which practices the evidence supports. Two common ones are worth knowing about. Routine spinal X-rays are one. A 2018 review in a chiropractic journal concluded that X-rays "should not be routinely performed in chiropractic practice," because the radiation, overdiagnosis and cost usually outweigh the benefits.[^12] Ongoing "maintenance" visits are the other, and here the picture is more mixed. A 2018 trial found that for people with recurring back pain who had responded well to chiropractic, scheduled maintenance visits led to about 13 fewer days of bothersome pain over a year. They also involved more treatments.[^13] That's a real benefit for a specific group, and it doesn't support lifelong adjustments for people who have no symptoms.

## The question

> What does chiropractic add that exercise and physical therapy don't, and what's the evidence beyond back and neck pain?

For back pain, it mostly adds options and access rather than better results. Manipulation performs about as well as other recommended treatments, and physical therapists and osteopathic physicians can offer it too.[^7][^4] Some people will prefer a chiropractor's hands-on approach, and that's a reasonable choice.

Beyond back and neck pain, the answer is "not much, so far." Some headaches show promise. For asthma, colic and painful periods, manipulation did no better than a sham.[^8] If a chiropractor offers to treat those, or says your spine is the root of your general health, the evidence doesn't back them.

This post isn't medical advice. Talk to a doctor about new or severe pain, especially pain that comes with numbness, weakness or fever.

## The thinking tool: "I felt better afterward"

Back pain often gets better on its own. The physicians' guideline says most people with acute or subacute low back pain "improve over time regardless of treatment."[^5] So feeling better after an adjustment is exactly what you'd expect, whether or not the adjustment did anything.

Assuming that because B came after A, A caused B, is called the *post hoc* fallacy. The cure is a comparison group. That's why trials compare manipulation with sham treatments and with other therapies, rather than just asking whether patients improved.
