## The claim

Somewhere in the forests of the Pacific Northwest, the story goes, lives a large, undiscovered primate: upright, hair-covered, seven or eight feet tall, and very good at avoiding us. Believers point to a long record of footprints, eyewitness reports, and footage, most famously the Patterson–Gimlin film, as evidence that a real animal sits behind the legend.

It's a claim worth taking seriously, because it's a *testable* one. A breeding population of large mammals leaves traces. So the question isn't whether Bigfoot is a fun story. It's whether those traces exist.

## Why it's compelling

The appeal is easy to understand. North America really does have vast tracts of forest that few people visit, and new species really are still being described. Thousands of people have reported encounters, many of them sincere, sober, and shaken by what they saw. And the idea of a living relative hiding in the woods is a wonderful thing to want to be true.

The name itself has a concrete origin. In August 1958, a bulldozer operator for Wallace Construction in Humboldt County, California, found huge humanlike footprints around his equipment. The *Humboldt Times* ran the story on its front page and coined the word "Bigfoot."[^1]

## What the evidence shows

**The footprints have a complicated history.** After Ray L. Wallace died in 2002, his family said he'd had a friend carve a pair of 16-inch wooden feet, and that he and his brother wore them to make the prints as a prank.[^1] That's the family's account, and it doesn't explain every track ever cast. But it does show that the founding piece of evidence was, at the very least, easy to fake, and the same report describes pranks and hoaxes as "just part of Mr. Wallace's nature."[^1]

**The DNA comes back as known animals.** In 2014, geneticist Bryan Sykes and colleagues ran the first systematic genetic survey of hair samples attributed to "anomalous primates," including Bigfoot, the Yeti, and similar creatures. After rigorous decontamination they sequenced 30 hair samples. Every one came from a known mammal. The two most surprising results, a pair of Himalayan samples, matched an ancient polar bear, not a primate.[^2] This is exactly the kind of test that *could* have found something new. It didn't.

**Sightings track bears.** In 2009, researchers built an ecological niche model, the kind of habitat-prediction model biologists use for real species, from a large database of reported Bigfoot sightings and footprints in western North America. The predicted "Bigfoot habitat" closely matched the range of the American black bear. The authors concluded that many sightings may be cases of mistaken identity.[^3] A later study extended the analysis to all of the US and Canada. After adjusting for human population and forest area, it found that Bigfoot reports rise with black bear populations: roughly a 4% increase in sightings for every additional 1,000 bears.[^4] <!-- VERIFY: the 4% / per-1,000-bears figures are from the preprint abstract; confirm they match the published Journal of Zoology version --> A bear standing on its hind legs at dusk, glimpsed for a few seconds, is a very plausible "upright hairy figure."

**The usual candidate ancestor lived on the wrong continent, in the wrong era.** Bigfoot is sometimes linked to *Gigantopithecus blacki*, the largest primate known to have existed. But the fossil evidence places it in southern China, where it went extinct between about 295,000 and 215,000 years ago as its forest habitat changed.[^5] A giant ape in the fossil record is real. A giant ape in Washington State today is a separate claim that needs its own evidence.

## The question

> If a large primate exists in North America, why has no confirmed physical evidence — bones, tissue, or definitive DNA — ever been recovered?

The most economical answer is that there's no such animal. A population large enough to survive for generations would include many animals, and those animals would die, get hit by cars, and leave bodies, bones, and teeth for scavengers to scatter and hikers to find. That's how we know about the elusive animals that really do share those forests. Meanwhile, the physical evidence that *has* been tested points the other way: hair samples turn out to be from known species,[^2] and sighting reports follow the distribution of a large, familiar mammal that sometimes walks on two legs.[^3][^4]

None of this proves that every witness is lying, and we shouldn't suggest it. Most people who report a sighting saw *something*. The evidence just suggests that the something was usually a bear, occasionally a hoax, and sometimes the brain's eagerness to resolve a dim, moving shape into a face.

## The thinking tool: absence of evidence, when evidence is expected

You'll often hear that "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence." Sometimes that's true. If nobody has looked, finding nothing tells you little. But when a claim *predicts* evidence, and people have looked hard for decades without finding it, the absence starts to count.

A large breeding population of primates predicts carcasses, skeletons, roadkill, and DNA in hair snags. Decades of searching, and the best-designed genetic test to date,[^2] have produced none of these. That's not proof of impossibility, and a single specimen would overturn it overnight. That's the beauty of a testable claim. Until then, the reasonable position is to stay curious, keep looking at the evidence, and follow it where it leads. Right now, it leads to bears.
